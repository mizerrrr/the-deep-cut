Free, legal music on the Internet Archive.

The Internet Archive is one of the largest collections of free music on the web, and one of the hardest to browse. Here is where to start, and how to tell what you're allowed to do with each album.

Published 29 October 2026 · 6 min read

The Internet Archive (archive.org) is a nonprofit digital library. Besides books, films and saved web pages, it hosts a huge amount of audio: albums released by artists under open licenses, the catalogs of online labels, live concert recordings, digitized records and much more. You can stream almost all of it in the browser, and download much of it, without an account.

The catch is discovery. There is no "new releases" shelf and no editor picking highlights. Search results are long and mixed with podcasts, radio shows and lectures. These are the collections worth knowing.

The collections to know

Netlabels

The Netlabels collection (collection:netlabels) preserves the catalogs of online-only labels that release music for free download, much of it electronic, ambient, experimental and lo-fi. Because each label curates its own catalog, it is one of the most rewarding places to dig. Read more in What is a netlabel?

Jamendo albums

Jamendo is a platform where independent artists publish music under Creative Commons licenses. The Archive keeps a large set of those albums (collection:jamendo-albums) across every genre, from rock and pop to soundtrack and world music.

Free Music Archive

The Free Music Archive (collection:freemusicarchive) is a library of legal, mostly Creative Commons music, built with input from radio curators. Expect indie, folk, hip-hop, electronic and experimental releases.

Live Music Archive

The Live Music Archive (collection:etree) holds concert recordings from bands that allow fans to record and share their shows. If you like live, taper-friendly bands, it can keep you busy for years.

Digitized records and out-of-print releases

The Archive also holds digitized LPs, 78s and collections such as Unlocked Recordings, built around releases that are hard to find elsewhere. Rights here are different from Creative Commons music: read each item's page before reusing anything.

Regional collections

Some of the best surprises are regional: Brazilian and Latin American releases, Colombian música campesina and other collections built by people preserving their local music. Searching by subject (for example subject:mpb or subject:cumbia) helps find them.

How to search without drowning

  • Limit to audio. Add mediatype:audio to any search on archive.org, or use the Audio filter on the left of the results.
  • Pick a collection. Combine it with a collection, for example mediatype:audio AND collection:netlabels.
  • Sort with intent. Sorting by downloads shows what others found; sorting by date added shows what just arrived.
  • Filter by subject and year. Subjects are tags written by uploaders, so they're uneven, but genre words still narrow things a lot.
  • Skip the noise. Titles with "podcast", "episode", "radio" or "lecture" are rarely albums.

Or skip the searching altogether: The Deep Cut samples these collections at random and shows six albums at a time, already checked to be real albums with at least four tracks and an original cover.

How to read the license

Every item page on archive.org shows its license or rights statement, usually in the right-hand column. "Free to listen" and "free to reuse" are not the same thing. The most common Creative Commons licenses mean:

  • CC0 / Public domain: no restrictions. Use it however you like.
  • CC BY: reuse freely, including commercially, as long as you credit the artist.
  • CC BY-SA: as CC BY, but anything you make with it must be shared under the same license.
  • NC (NonCommercial): no commercial use, which includes most monetized videos.
  • ND (NoDerivatives): share it as it is; no remixes, edits or use as a soundtrack.

When an item shows no license, assume all rights are reserved: listening on archive.org is fine, but don't reuse it in your own work. If you plan to use a track in a video or a podcast, check the license on the item page first and credit the artist every time.

Support the artists and the Archive

Free doesn't mean the music has no value. If you love an album, look the artist up: many sell their music on Bandcamp or their own sites. And if the Archive helped you find it, consider donating to the Internet Archive, which keeps all of this online.
