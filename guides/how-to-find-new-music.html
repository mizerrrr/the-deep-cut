How to find new music without algorithms.

If your recommendations keep serving the same five artists, the problem isn't your taste. It's the loop. Here are nine ways to step out of it.

Published 29 October 2026 · 7 min read

Streaming recommendations are built to predict what you will play to the end. That makes them very good at giving you more of what you already like, and not very good at surprising you. They learn from your history, so every song you skip and every song you finish narrows the next suggestion a little further. After a few years, many listeners feel they have "heard everything", when they have really heard the same corner of music many times.

Finding new music again means adding sources that don't know your history. None of these methods needs a subscription.

1. Let chance choose

The quickest way to break a pattern is to remove the pattern. Random discovery tools pick music with no idea of what you played yesterday, so you hear things no recommendation would have reached.

That is the idea behind The Deep Cut: every search shows six random albums from the Internet Archive's music collections, each in a different style, with a player built in. Most will not be for you. One of them might become a favorite, and you would never have found it any other way. Give each album the length of one or two tracks, then move on without guilt.

2. Listen to whole albums, not playlists

Playlists are built from singles, and singles are the most familiar-sounding part of any record. Album tracks, the "deep cuts", are where artists take risks. When something catches your ear, play the full album it came from. You will learn far more about whether you like an artist from track seven than from the hit.

3. Dig by label, not by artist

Record labels, especially small ones, are curators. A label with a sound you like has usually released ten other artists you will like too. When you find an album you love, look up who released it and go through the rest of their catalog.

Online-only netlabels are especially good for this: their full catalogs are often free to stream and download, and many are preserved on the Internet Archive.

4. Follow people instead of feeds

A person with taste beats a model trained on everyone. Find a few people whose ears you trust and follow what they play:

  • Freeform and community radio. Stations such as WFMU, KEXP or NTS have DJs who choose their own music, with archives of past shows online. Your local community or college station counts too.
  • Music writers and newsletters. Editorial sites like Bandcamp Daily write about records the charts ignore.
  • Record store staff. Ask what they have been listening to, not what is selling.
  • Friends. Swap one album a week with someone whose taste is different from yours.

5. Travel by decade and place

Pick a country and a decade you know nothing about, and listen. Brazilian music from the 1970s, Colombian campesino music, Japanese city pop from the 1980s, West African guitar records: each is a world of its own. On The Deep Cut you can narrow the random picks to one era, from the 1960s to 2025–26, and let the place surprise you.

6. Read the credits

Producers, session musicians, engineers and featured artists connect records that algorithms treat as unrelated. If a drummer or producer appears on two albums you love, look up everything else they worked on. Databases like Discogs list credits for millions of releases.

7. Join a listening community

Forums and communities built around discovery, such as r/listentothis on Reddit (for lesser-known artists) or the user charts on Rate Your Music, are full of people sharing what they found. Read their recommendations for a genre you don't usually play.

8. Go to the source: archives and libraries

Huge amounts of music sit in public archives that nobody browses. The Internet Archive holds netlabel releases, Creative Commons albums, digitized LPs and live recordings, free to stream. It's a fantastic place to dig, but hard to browse, which is why we wrote a guide to free, legal music on the Internet Archive. Public libraries often offer free music streaming too, and still lend CDs and records.

9. Keep a log

Discovery is easy to lose. Save every album that grabs you, even half-way, and come back to it a week later. On The Deep Cut you can save albums and like tracks with no account; they stay in your browser. A short list of "maybes" is how a one-time accident becomes a habit.

A simple weekly routine

  1. Spend ten minutes on random albums and save anything that holds your attention.
  2. Pick one saved album and listen to it in full, start to finish.
  3. Look up its label or its credits and add one related record to your list.
  4. Listen to one radio show or newsletter pick outside your usual genres.

Algorithms aren't the enemy: they're good at "more like this". Just don't let them be your only source. Add one source that doesn't know you, and the music gets bigger again.
